About

The researcher.
The editor.
The storyteller.

I've always been drawn to what doesn't make it into the official record. The oral traditions preserved across generations in kitchens and church yards. The folk practices that outlasted the systems that tried to erase them. The cultural memory that lives not in archives but in bodies and breath and repetition. This is where my research begins.

My academic work sits at the intersection of literary studies, rhetoric, folklore, and digital humanities. I'm finishing an M.A. in Literary Studies at UCF, where I'm also completing certificates in Professional Writing and Digital Humanities in the Age of AI. But my real education started long before graduate school. It started in libraries.

I've spent years teaching people how to research: how to find the thread in a body of material, how to ask a better question, how to build something coherent from scattered sources. As the first library employee at South College's Orlando campus, I built the program from the ground up, and I've watched what happens when someone who couldn't find an answer suddenly can. That moment matters to me. It's why I do this work.

Outside the library, I'm a fiction writer. I'm developing a literary magical realism novel set in an alternate-history Brooklyn rooted in Black folklore and diasporic spiritual traditions. The novel is my deepest research project and my most personal one. It lives at the center of everything I'm thinking about: how we carry history in our bodies, what gets handed down, what gets buried, and what rises anyway.

I believe the most interesting stories are the ones that have been waiting the longest to be told. My job, whether I'm working with a manuscript or a research question or a community program, is to find that waiting story and give it room to breathe.

I'm building toward a career that lets me do this work from anywhere. If you're looking for someone who can hold complexity, find the thread, and bring the thing home, I'd love to talk.

Lana Carter

Work & Expertise

Three ways I work.

01

Developmental Editing & Narrative Consulting

I work with writers at the level of story architecture: structure, character arc, thematic coherence, and what the narrative is actually trying to do beneath the surface.

My research background in folklore, literary history, and cultural theory means I can meet manuscripts in territory that demands specificity. Historical fiction, literary nonfiction, stories rooted in cultural traditions that deserve more than a surface reading. I give feedback that takes the work seriously because I take the writer seriously.

I'm particularly suited to projects where the research layer and the story layer need to cohere, and where getting the details right is inseparable from getting the story right.

02

Library Services, Research & Information Literacy

I've built a library program from scratch, which means I understand every layer: reference and research support, collection development, instructional design, digital resource management, and the particular skill of meeting a patron exactly where they are.

At South College's Orlando campus, I was the first library employee. I designed the instructional program, created patron-facing guides and training materials, and built systems meant to grow. My background in public library services means I'm equally at home with wide-ranging patron needs and the beautiful unpredictability of a public-facing environment.

I'm drawn to roles where the work involves building something: a program, a curriculum, a knowledge base that didn't fully exist before.

03

Cultural Programming & Content Strategy

There is a particular skill in translating complex ideas for the audiences who need them most and don't have time for academic register. I've been doing that translation my whole career.

I develop programming and content at the intersection of libraries, community organizations, and cultural institutions. I write institutional content that sounds like a human being wrote it. I create materials that a patron or audience member will actually read and use.

I'm especially interested in programming that takes community knowledge seriously: oral histories, folk traditions, and the stories that institutions often overlook in favor of what's already on the shelf.

Now

What I'm working on.

I'm finishing my M.A. in Literary Studies at UCF, where my research has been pulling me deeper into the intersections of folklore methodology, narrative theory, and the particular ways that Black diasporic traditions encode and transmit knowledge. Most days I feel like I'm building a longer and longer reading list instead of a shorter one, which seems about right.

The novel is alive and ongoing. It's a literary magical realism story set in an alternate-history Brooklyn, rooted in Black folklore and diasporic spiritual traditions, asking what a place carries differently when certain histories are allowed to survive. I'm reading widely in Black diasporic literature and folk traditions to feed it. The research and the writing are deeply tangled, which is exactly how I want it.

I'm building out my Substack, where I write about folklore, mythology, narrative craft, and the hidden histories that keep surfacing no matter how hard anyone tried to bury them. I'm building toward a location-independent career that lets this work go wherever it needs to go. If something here resonates, I'd genuinely love to hear from you.
